FODs#

Spherical deconvolution estimates a fibre orientation distribution (FOD) in each voxel, which says how much of the voxel’s fibres run in each direction. It needs a response function, the signal of a single bundle of fibres that all run the same way. The signal of a voxel is modelled as a sum of copies of this response, each turned along one fibre direction and weighted by the share of the fibres that run that way. The fit finds the weights that best reproduce the measured signal, and these weights are the FOD. Larmor stores FODs as spherical harmonic (SH) coefficients. Citing lists the papers of the methods, and Conventions gives the SH bases.

The data on this page

The examples on this page run on a small synthetic image, which the code below writes to the current folder as dwi.nii.gz, with its gradient files dwi.bval and dwi.bvec and a mask, mask.nii.gz. The image has 12 x 12 x 2 voxels of 2 mm, two b=0 images and 30 directions at each of b=1000 and 2000 s/mm^2. Two bundles cross in its middle, one along x and one along y, and the other voxels hold isotropic diffusion. To work on your own data, use your files instead.

import numpy as np

from larmor.io import save_volume, write_bvals_bvecs
from larmor.sd import AnisotropicRF
from larmor.types import GradientTable, Volume

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
directions = rng.normal(size=(60, 3))
directions /= np.linalg.norm(directions, axis=1, keepdims=True)
bvals = np.r_[0, 0, [1000] * 30, [2000] * 30]
bvecs = np.r_[np.zeros((2, 3)), directions]
gtab = GradientTable(bvals, bvecs)

fibre = AnisotropicRF([1.7e-3, 0.3e-3, 0.3e-3], mean_kurtosis=0.5)
along_x, along_y = fibre.signal(bvals, gtab.bvecs, np.eye(3)[:2]).T
isotropic = np.exp(-bvals * 0.8e-3)

signal = np.broadcast_to(isotropic, (12, 12, 2, len(bvals))).copy()
signal[:, 4:8] = along_x
signal[4:8, :] = along_y
signal[4:8, 4:8] = (along_x + along_y) / 2
data = 1000 * signal + rng.normal(0, 10, signal.shape)

affine = np.diag([2.0, 2.0, 2.0, 1.0])
save_volume(Volume(data.astype(np.float32), affine), "dwi.nii.gz")
write_bvals_bvecs(gtab, "dwi.bval", "dwi.bvec", ref_path="dwi.nii.gz")
save_volume(Volume(np.ones((12, 12, 2), np.uint8), affine), "mask.nii.gz")

Fitting FODs#

The fit always needs a white matter response. Larmor estimates it from the voxels with FA > 0.7, or you can give its eigenvalues yourself. Isotropic responses for grey matter and CSF are optional. With them, the fit is multi-tissue and also gives the fraction of each voxel that each tissue fills.

from larmor.io import load_dwi, load_volume, save_volume
from larmor.sd import SDModel, build_response_functions

dwi, gtab = load_dwi("dwi.nii.gz", "dwi.bval", "dwi.bvec")
mask = load_volume("mask.nii.gz")
responses = build_response_functions(
    dwi, gtab, mask, gm_diffusivity=0.7e-3, csf_diffusivity=3e-3
)
fit = SDModel(responses, lmax=8).fit(dwi, gtab, mask=mask)

save_volume(dwi.with_data(fit.fods[0]), "fod.nii.gz")
save_volume(dwi.with_data(fit.tissue_fractions), "fractions.nii.gz")
print(f"lmax {fit.lmax}, basis {fit.basis}, shape {fit.fods[0].shape}")

build_response_functions returns the responses in the order white matter, grey matter, CSF. Without gm_diffusivity and csf_diffusivity it returns the white matter response alone, for a single-tissue fit, and wm_eigen_vals=[1.7e-3, 0.2e-3, 0.2e-3] gives the white matter response instead of estimating it.

lar fod dwi.nii.gz mask.nii.gz fod --bvals dwi.bval --bvecs dwi.bvec

The command writes the FOD to fod.nii.gz and the tissue fractions to fod_tissue_fraction.nii.gz. By default it fits white matter, grey matter (isotropic, 0.7e-3 mm^2/s, with GRL) and CSF (3e-3 mm^2/s). --gm-rf none and --csf-rf none leave grey matter and CSF out, and --wm-rf 1.7e-3 0.2e-3 0.2e-3 gives the white matter eigenvalues instead of estimating them.

The FODs are in world coordinates. A fit with even degrees up to lmax has (lmax + 1)(lmax + 2) / 2 coefficients, which is 28 for degree 6 and 45 for degree 8. The fit starts at lmax (8 by default) and lowers it until there are no more coefficients than directions in the highest shell, so the data on this page, with 30 directions per shell, give degree 6.

GRL and mFOD can use any of three solvers. DRL is damped Richardson-Lucy, L2NNLS is non-negative least squares with a ridge penalty (reg 0.1 by default), and Lasso is an elastic net (reg 0.01 and alpha 0.9 by default; Tibshirani 1996; Zou and Hastie 2005). With alpha 0.9, the elastic net puts 90% of its penalty on the L1 norm, which favours sparse FODs, and 10% on the L2 norm. It fits no intercept, since the FOD model has no constant term. In a multi-tissue fit, the elastic net fits the signal left after the isotropic tissues are subtracted, and the tissue fractions come from a separate fit in which the FOD’s signal is normalised, so the scale of the elastic-net FOD does not set them. A stronger penalty can merge fibres that cross at small angles and drop the weaker fibre of a crossing, while a weaker one lets more noise into the FOD. The solvers find the FOD on 300 fixed directions, where DRL and L2NNLS give non-negative values, while the elastic net can give negative ones.

mFOD can also model grey matter with an anisotropic response, the NODDI response (NoddiRF; Zhang et al. 2012), and then gives a second FOD for grey matter:

from larmor.sd import L2NNLS, SDMethod

responses = build_response_functions(
    dwi, gtab, mask, gm_noddi=True, csf_diffusivity=3e-3
)
model = SDModel(responses, method=SDMethod.MFOD, solver=L2NNLS(reg=0.1))
fit = model.fit(dwi, gtab, mask=mask)
print(len(fit.fods), "FODs")

lar fod dwi.nii.gz mask.nii.gz mfod --bvals dwi.bval --bvecs dwi.bvec \
    --method mfod

With mFOD, grey matter defaults to the NODDI response (--gm-rf noddi) and the solver to L2NNLS. This writes one FOD per anisotropic response, mfod_fod1.nii.gz (white matter) and mfod_fod2.nii.gz (grey matter), and mfod_tissue_fraction.nii.gz.

inner_shell_weight (--inner-shell-weight) changes the weight of the measurements outside the highest shell in a multi-tissue fit.

GRL and mFOD find the FOD’s values on 300 fixed directions and then fit SH coefficients to those values. With the L2NNLS and Lasso solvers, the l = 0 coefficient is then set to the sum of the values divided by sqrt(4 pi), so that the FOD’s integral does not depend on the direction of the fibre.

The FOD on the 300 directions is sharper than SH coefficients up to lmax 8 can hold, so the fitted FOD shows rings around each lobe, about 51 degrees away from it and at about 6 to 8% of its height. Where two fibres cross, the rings of the two fibres add up to about 12 to 14% of the main lobes, in four places around the crossing. A peak search with a relative threshold of 0.1 would report these as four extra fibres. The FODs of GRL and mFOD therefore use a relative threshold of 0.2: fit.peak_relative_threshold is 0.2 for them, and lar peaks uses 0.2 unless --fod-method says the FOD comes from CSD or MSMT-CSD. In simulations this removed every ring peak and kept the smaller fibre of 70/30 and 80/20 crossings, but a real fibre below a fifth of the largest one in its voxel is dropped too. CSD and MSMT-CSD fit the SH coefficients directly, do not show these rings, and keep the threshold of 0.1.

Constrained spherical deconvolution#

CSD (CSDModel) fits only the shell with the highest b-value, with one white matter response. The response is given by its zonal SH coefficients (l = 0, 2, 4, …) in the units of the signal. The fit keeps the FOD from going negative by penalising it, on 300 directions, wherever it is negative. This constraint lets the fit use a higher lmax than the shell’s directions alone would support, so CSD keeps the lmax it is given. The signal is not divided by the b=0 signal, so the FOD’s integral is the voxel’s signal relative to the response’s. CSDSolver holds the solver’s settings.

Without a response, the fit estimates one from the single-fibre voxels of the mask with estimate_csd_rf(). It repeatedly deconvolves the voxels, keeps the 300 whose largest FOD peak is large and whose second peak is small, and fits a new response to their signals, until the selected voxels no longer change.

from larmor.sd import CSDModel, estimate_csd_rf

estimate = estimate_csd_rf(dwi, gtab, mask, num_voxels=50)
print(estimate.rf.coefficients, estimate.voxels.sum(), "voxels")
fit = CSDModel(estimate.rf, lmax=8).fit(dwi, gtab, mask=mask)
save_volume(dwi.with_data(fit.fods[0]), "csd.nii.gz")

CSDModel(lmax=8) without a response estimates it in fit with the defaults. To use a response file written by MRtrix3’s dwi2response, read it with read_mrtrix_response() and give it the b-values of the data’s shells with build_csd_response(), as in CSDModel(build_csd_response(read_mrtrix_response("response.txt"), gtab)).

lar fod dwi.nii.gz mask.nii.gz csd --bvals dwi.bval --bvecs dwi.bvec --method csd

This estimates the response in the mask and writes the FOD to csd.nii.gz. CSD gives no tissue fractions. --response response.txt gives a response in MRtrix3’s format instead.

Multi-shell multi-tissue CSD#

MSMTCSDModel is multi-shell multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution (MSMT-CSD; Jeurissen et al. 2014). It fits a white matter FOD and the amounts of isotropic grey matter and CSF to every measurement of a voxel at once, b = 0 included. Each tissue has a response, a ZonalSHRF, given by its zonal SH coefficients on every shell in the units of the signal; the grey matter and CSF responses are isotropic (lmax 0). The FOD is kept non-negative on 300 directions, and the isotropic amounts are kept non-negative. Three tissues need at least three distinct b-values, b = 0 among them.

You can read the responses from files written by MRtrix3’s dwi2response with read_mrtrix_response() (one row per shell), with the shells’ b-values from response_shells(). Or you can estimate them from the data and a brain mask with estimate_tissue_responses(), which needs no tissue segmentation (Dhollander et al. 2016, 2019). MSMTCSDModel() without responses estimates all three in fit. The estimate first erodes the mask by three voxels. The data on this page have only two slices, so the example below does not erode the mask, and since the data hold a single isotropic tissue, it fits white matter and CSF only.

import numpy as np

from larmor.sd import MSMTCSDModel, estimate_tissue_responses

responses = estimate_tissue_responses(dwi, gtab, mask, erode=0)
model = MSMTCSDModel([responses.wm, responses.csf], lmax=8)
fit = model.fit(dwi, gtab, mask=mask)
print(fit.lmax, fit.tissue_fractions.shape)

np.savetxt("wm.txt", responses.wm.coefficients)
np.savetxt("csf.txt", responses.csf.coefficients)

fit.fods[0] is the white matter FOD. Its integral, c00 sqrt(4 pi), is the white matter’s share of the signal relative to its response. fit.tissue_fractions holds these shares for every tissue, white matter first, normalised to sum to 1.

lar fod dwi.nii.gz mask.nii.gz msmt --bvals dwi.bval --bvecs dwi.bvec --method msmt-csd
lar fod dwi.nii.gz mask.nii.gz msmt --bvals dwi.bval --bvecs dwi.bvec --method msmt-csd \
    --wm-response wm.txt --gm-response gm.txt --csf-response csf.txt --force

The first command estimates the three responses from the data and the mask, and the second reads them from files such as those dwi2response dhollander writes. Both write the white matter FOD to msmt.nii.gz and the fractions to msmt_tissue_fraction.nii.gz.

SH bases#

Larmor reads and writes three real SH bases, which hold the same functions in a different order, with different signs or in a different frame. They are "tournier", the default and the basis of MRtrix3, "descoteaux", and "leemans", the basis of ExploreDTI and MRIToolkit. For Tournier and Descoteaux, Larmor supports the current versions of the bases, not the legacy ones.

An image file does not record its basis, so you must give the basis whenever an FOD is read: with basis= in Python, and with --basis on the command line (lar fod, lar peaks, lar track). A wrong basis gives wrong directions without any error. The fit can write its FODs in another basis with SDModel(..., basis="leemans") or lar fod --basis leemans, and existing FODs are converted as follows:

from larmor.io import load_volume, save_volume
from larmor.sd import convert_sh_basis, sh_layout

fod = load_volume("fod.nii.gz")
leemans = convert_sh_basis(fod.data, "tournier", "leemans")
save_volume(fod.with_data(leemans), "fod_leemans.nii.gz")
print(sh_layout(fod.data.shape[-1]))

sh_layout() returns the degree implied by the number of coefficients, and whether all degrees are stored, as for an asymmetric FOD.

lar sh-convert fod.nii.gz fod_leemans.nii.gz --from tournier --to leemans
